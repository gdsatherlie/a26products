What is MOQ, and how does it actually affect your cost?

MOQ means minimum order quantity: the smallest order a supplier wants to produce at given terms. Teams often treat MOQ as a fixed wall. In practice, MOQ is usually a negotiation around setup cost recovery, capacity planning, and risk allocation.

  • MOQ vs unit price curve
  • Negotiation levers

MOQ meaning in plain English

Suppliers set MOQ because each production run has fixed overhead: setup time, material handling, planning, and quality checks. At low quantities, these overhead costs make unit economics unattractive. Your job is to decide whether paying a higher unit cost for lower MOQ is safer than buying more inventory than you can sell.

Negotiation levers that often work

  • Phase-in MOQ: smaller first order with committed reorder trigger.
  • Variant consolidation: fewer colors/SKUs in first batch.
  • Shared materials: align with supplier’s existing material stock.
  • Schedule flexibility: accept longer lead time for lower MOQ.
  • Deposit structure: stronger deposit can reduce supplier risk perception.

Common mistakes

  • Optimizing only for unit price while ignoring unsold inventory risk.
  • Accepting MOQ without understanding setup assumptions.
  • Skipping cash-flow modeling for 90-180 day sales window.

Questions to ask a factory

  • What exactly drives your MOQ for this product?
  • Can MOQ be split across variants or delivery windows?
  • What quantity unlocks the next unit-price tier?
  • How does MOQ change if we commit to a reorder schedule?
