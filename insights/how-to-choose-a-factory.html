Plain-English definition

Choosing a factory is a risk allocation decision, not only a sourcing step. You are selecting who will carry your specifications into repeatable output under time pressure.

Choosing a factory is a risk allocation decision, not only a sourcing step. You are selecting who will carry your specifications into repeatable output under time pressure. In operator practice, this should be reviewed at least weekly with a named owner and clear closure criteria, otherwise decisions degrade into assumptions and timeline risk accumulates.

How to run a capability-first selection process

Define non-negotiables first, then run a two-phase screen: paper screen for capability alignment, then stress test for communication and issue ownership. Normalize quotes, then score candidates with weighted criteria tied to your program risk profile.

What it costs / what drives cost

Lower quote prices can hide high downstream operating costs if quality controls are weak or process variation is high. Cost drivers include process complexity, revision frequency, material constraints, and communication reliability.

Common mistakes

Teams often treat references as proof, skip communication tests, and overweight speed of response over depth of response. Another mistake is failing to define what “acceptable” means before requesting samples.

Red flags

Quote assumptions not written, inability to identify escalation owner, overly generic quality language, and shifting responses to the same question are warning signs.

Questions to ask a factory

How do you control first-article variation? Which stage has highest defect risk? What changes require written approval? How are late material issues escalated?

FAQ

When should teams prioritize how to choose a factory: scoring rubric, proof tests, and communication stress checks?

As early as possible, before high-cost commitments and before timeline pressure reduces decision quality.

How detailed should documentation be?

Detailed enough that another operator can reconstruct what was decided, by whom, and why.

Can this be done with an existing supplier?

Yes. Most improvements are operating-system improvements, not supplier replacements.

What is the first practical next step?

Create a single-page decision checklist and run it in your next supplier status review.
