Manufacturing lead times explained: plan with reality, not optimism.

Lead-time misses usually come from optimistic assumptions and invisible dependencies, not bad luck. This guide shows how to build a practical lead-time model and where to place buffers that protect launch confidence.

In this guide

  • Lead-time breakdown
  • Critical path method
  • Planning worksheet
  • Common mistakes
  • Questions to ask factories

Lead-time breakdown

PhaseTypical durationDelay drivers
Supplier confirmation1–2 weeksUnresolved technical questions
Sampling3–8 weeksRevision loops and material swaps
Tooling/debug2–6 weeksRework and correction cycles
Production4–10 weeksCapacity conflicts and yield issues
QC/release1–2 weeksDefect closure and reinspection
Freight1–6 weeksMode choice and congestion

Critical path method in plain English

Your critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks that determines final completion date. If a task is off the critical path, delay may not move launch. If a task is on the critical path, even small delay cascades. Track this chain weekly, not monthly.

Lead Time Planning Worksheet

  • Define target launch date and immutable constraints.
  • List all major phases and dependencies in order.
  • Assign best-case, likely-case, and risk-case duration.
  • Add buffer where uncertainty is structurally high.
  • Set weekly review cadence with planned vs actual dates.

Common mistakes

  • Using best-case timeline as committed timeline.
  • Ignoring sample revision probability.
  • Treating inspection and defect closure as zero-time events.

Questions to ask factories

  • Which milestones are most likely to slip for this product type?
  • What are your current capacity constraints by month?
  • How do you communicate schedule risk before a miss?
